# Adapting the commentary to the type of IA prompt

Read this once the student has told you which IA prompt they chose. The commentary structure in `SKILL.md` stays the same for every prompt, but two steps depend on the prompt's shape: the **contrast or limit** step, and what the **link sentence** has to say. A prompt about "what counts as" something needs a criterion, while a prompt about "to what extent" needs a degree, and so on.

Numbers follow the 35-prompt list in the IB TOK guide used for recent assessment. Confirm the number and exact wording against the student's current guide, and always use the prompt's wording in the commentary. Prompts are paraphrased here by keywords.

Contents: 1 Prompt families · 2 What each family needs · 3 Choosing and checking · 4 Mismatches to catch

## 2. What each family needs

**A. Criteria and distinctions**
- *Theory first:* a cited textbook statement of the criterion or the categories.
- *Example from the object:* test one specific claim from the object against the criterion.
- *Contrast or limit:* say what a claim that fails the criterion looks like, or how it differs from the neighbouring category. If the prompt names several categories, show how each differs from the other two.
- *Link:* name the criterion. "We can tell [prompt wording] by asking whether…"
- *Watch for:* leaping from "the source is reputable" to "the claim passes the test".

**B. Extent, possibility and limits**
- *Theory first:* what would make this attainable or possible (for example, what certainty or objectivity would require).
- *Example from the object:* evidence from the object that pushes towards it, and evidence that limits it.
- *Contrast or limit:* explicitly give the limit. A one-sided answer to a "to what extent" prompt reads as an assertion.
- *Link:* give a degree, such as "to some extent, because… but not fully, because…", not just "yes" or "no".
- *Watch for:* absolute words ("always", "never", "completely") without evidence.

**C. Comparison**
- *Theory first:* the basis of comparison (the feature in the prompt: usefulness, reliability, openness to interpretation).
- *Example from the object:* one side of the comparison, shown through the object.
- *Contrast or limit:* a brief contrast case, from another type of knowledge, or another of the exhibition's objects, so the comparison is real.
- *Link:* state which side comes out ahead on that feature, and why.
- *Watch for:* describing only one type of knowledge and calling that a comparison.

**D. Influence, role and relationship**
- *Theory first:* a cited statement of the mechanism (how values, culture, experts, tools, imagination and so on shape knowledge).
- *Example from the object:* a specific place in the object where that influence can be seen, with evidence.
- *Contrast or limit:* where the influence doesn't apply, or an alternative explanation for what the object shows.
- *Link:* state the influence and its direction. "X shapes what we accept as knowledge by…"
- *Watch for:* asserting a causal link ("values distort the claim") without showing how it operates in the object.

**E. Ethical and purpose**
- *Theory first:* a cited ethical or purpose-based idea, such as obligations, harm, benefit, ownership.
- *Example from the object:* a specific case in the object with stakeholders and consequences.
- *Contrast or limit:* the strongest opposing view, and why the student still reaches their position.
- *Link:* a reasoned position in the prompt's wording ("Some knowledge should/should not be sought because…").
- *Watch for:* moral assertions with no reasons, and universal claims ("all knowledge") with a single example.

## 3. Choosing and checking

- **Check that the prompt suits the three objects.** If one object only fits half the prompt, say so, and let the student decide whether to change the object or the angle. The three objects should each answer the prompt in a different way (see the whole-exhibition checklist in `assessment-criteria.md`).
- **Vary the link sentences.** Within one exhibition, the three objects should not end on the same sentence with the nouns swapped.
- **Concepts and tools to prefer by family:** A uses evidence, justification, certainty and reason. B uses certainty, objectivity and truth. C uses evidence and values. D uses culture, values, power, language, imagination, memory, emotion. E uses values, responsibility and power.

## 4. Mismatches to catch

- A criteria prompt answered with a description of the object's history.
- A "to what extent" prompt answered "yes" with no limit.
- A comparison prompt with only one side shown.
- An influence prompt where the influence is claimed, not shown in the object.
- An ethical prompt with no opposing view.
- Theory that fits another prompt (for example, the student's theory is about certainty, but the prompt is about ownership).
